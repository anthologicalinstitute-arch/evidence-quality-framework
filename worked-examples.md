# Worked Examples

## Example 1: Illustrative Composite Score

Assume:

| Factor | Illustrative score |
|---|---:|
| Source reliability (\(q_s\)) | 0.90 |
| Freshness (\(q_t\)) | 0.80 |
| Corroboration (\(q_c\)) | 0.75 |
| Provenance and completeness (\(q_p\)) | 1.00 |

Calculation:

\[
Q_e = 0.90 \times 0.80 \times 0.75 \times 1.00 = 0.54
\]

**Interpretation:** Under the proposed multiplicative rule, the composite score is 0.54.

This score should not be interpreted as a 54% probability that the evidence is true.

## Example 2: Effect of a Lower Factor

Suppose the other factors remain unchanged, but freshness falls from 0.80 to 0.50.

\[
Q_e = 0.90 \times 0.50 \times 0.75 \times 1.00 = 0.3375
\]

The composite score decreases because one input decreased.

## Questions for Further Research

- Are the scoring criteria sufficiently clear for independent researchers to reproduce the scores?
- Are all four factors necessary?
- Does multiplication produce useful results when one factor is near zero?
- How sensitive are results to subjective score assignments?
- Does the composite add value beyond reviewing each factor separately?

These examples are illustrative, not empirical validation.
